How to Play Meowdoku: The Three Rules Explained

Meowdoku looks simple for about ten seconds, then the three rules start interacting. This page explains each rule with a diagram, shows how the controls work in the official app, and walks through the opening deductions of a real board.

Rule 1 — one cat per coloured region

The board is divided into coloured territories of different shapes. Every territory needs exactly one cat: no empty territory, no territory with two. A one-tile region is a free placement; a large sprawling region is where elimination does the work.

Rule 2 — one cat per row and column

A cat controls its entire row and column. Place one and every other tile in those two lines becomes impossible, no matter which colour it belongs to.

Rule 3 — cats cannot touch, even diagonally

The eight tiles around a cat are forbidden: four orthogonal neighbours and four diagonal corners. This is the rule that catches most beginners, because a diagonal contact looks harmless on a wide board.

Hearts and level flow

Each level gives you three hearts. A wrong cat placement removes one; run out and the level restarts. Because the game punishes guessing, the correct habit is to place a cat only when a tile is logically forced. Use marks to record what you have eliminated, not guesses.

Boards start small and grow as you progress. The daily puzzle gives everyone the same new board each day and feeds a global fastest-time leaderboard.

Our original 6×6 practice board, solved. Colours are regions; each cat is a solution cell.

  1. Count the regions and find the smallest. The three-cell strip in the leftmost column (rows 4–6) is the smallest territory.
  2. Claim its line. That region must put its cat in the leftmost column, so no other region may use that column.
  3. Follow the consequence. The four-cell region at the top-left has two cells in the leftmost column; both are now dead. Its cat must be in the second column.
  4. Chain it once more. The second column is now claimed too. The bottom region has three cells in the second column and one in the third; all three second-column cells are dead, so its only remaining cell — row 6, column 3 — is forced.
  5. Mark the halo. Cross out the row, column and eight neighbours of every cat you place. The next forced tile usually appears at the edge of that halo.

You do not need to see the whole solution at once. Each forced cat removes possibilities, and the board slowly collapses. The strategy guide covers the standard deduction patterns in order.

Five beginner mistakes

  1. Guessing to “test” a tile. With three hearts, a guess is expensive. Mark it instead and keep deducing.
  2. Forgetting diagonals. The corner rule kills more candidates than any other constraint.
  3. Ignoring large regions. Big regions look unconstrained, but every cell outside them is a constraint on where they can go.
  4. Not clearing a region when it is solved. Once a region has its cat, all its other tiles are dead; mark them so you do not revisit them.
  5. Playing too fast for the leaderboard. Speed comes from clean notation; guessing for a fast time usually ends in a lost heart.

Frequently asked questions

How do you play Meowdoku?

Place one cat in every coloured region, one per row and one per column, and never let two cats touch — not even diagonally.

What happens if you make a mistake in Meowdoku?

You lose one of three hearts. Lose all three and the level restarts.

Do cats touch diagonally in Meowdoku?

No. Diagonal contact is illegal, just like sharing an edge.

How big are Meowdoku boards?

They start small and grow as you progress through the levels; the daily puzzle uses its own board.

Is there a daily Meowdoku puzzle?

Yes. The app adds a new daily puzzle and tracks fastest completion times on a global leaderboard.
