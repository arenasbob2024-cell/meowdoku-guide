Meowdoku Strategy: 7 Deduction Techniques

Meowdoku is solved by forced moves, not by inspiration. These seven patterns cover almost every deduction you need, in the order a strong player usually applies them. All diagrams come from our own practice boards, not from Oakever levels.

1. Start with the smallest region

Small regions have fewer possible homes, so they produce the first forced cat. Count the tiles in each colour and begin with the shortest list. A one-tile region is an immediate cat; a two- or three-tile region usually collapses after one row or column is blocked.

2. A region confined to one line claims that line

If every tile of a region sits in a single row or column, that line belongs to the region. No other colour may place a cat there. This is the fastest way to open a board with a long, thin region.

3. Cross out the whole row and column

After every cat, mark the rest of its row and column. It feels wasteful to mark ten tiles at once, but those marks are the deduction: they turn a big board into a small one. Do it consistently and you will stop re-checking the same cells.

4. Mark the eight-neighbour halo

The no-touching rule is what separates Meowdoku from a plain Queens puzzle. Every cat kills up to eight nearby tiles. Mark them immediately — especially the diagonals, which are easy to forget and expensive to miss.

5. Use region + line intersections

Combine constraints one at a time. If a region has cells in three rows but two of those rows already contain cats, the region’s cat must be in the third row. If that leaves only one cell in the third row, it is forced. Most mid-game moves are exactly this intersection.

6. Look for two-region pairs

When two regions together occupy only two rows, those two rows are closed to every other region: the pair must place both of its cats there. The same logic works for two columns. This pattern unlocks boards that look stuck after the easy cats are gone.

7. Never guess — use a mark

A guess costs a heart and teaches you nothing when it fails. If a cell looks promising but is not forced, mark it as “maybe” and keep deducing elsewhere. On paper and in our practice board you can mark freely; in the app, use the single-tap marker. The board eventually forces the cell, and you keep all three hearts.

Putting it together: an opening sequence

  1. Count region sizes; find the smallest.
  2. Check whether it is confined to one row or column; if so, claim that line.
  3. Place the cat, then mark its row, column and halo.
  4. Re-count the remaining candidates in each region. A region with one candidate is forced.
  5. Repeat. When no region has one candidate, look for a two-region pair or a region confined by occupied lines.

Speed and the leaderboard

Fast solvers are not guessing faster; they are marking less. Good notation means you never re-derive the same elimination. If you are chasing a leaderboard time, practise the patterns until they are automatic, then work on board scanning: sweep rows, columns and regions in the same order every time so a missed candidate stands out.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best way to start a Meowdoku puzzle?

Find the smallest coloured region and check whether it is confined to one row or column. That usually produces the first forced cat.

Why do I keep losing hearts in Meowdoku?

Guessing. Only place a cat when a tile is logically forced; use the single-tap marker to record eliminations instead.

What is the most missed rule in Meowdoku?

Diagonal contact. Cats cannot touch at corners, so every placement also blocks eight neighbouring tiles.

Does the practice board use Meowdoku levels?

No. Every practice puzzle is generated for this site with a verified unique solution; no Oakever levels or assets are used.
